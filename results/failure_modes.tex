\section*{What I tried}

I have this problem at work. We change a system prompt, nobody deletes the
conversation, and the model keeps following an instruction a user gave three
turns ago --- \emph{always reply in lowercase} --- because it is still sitting
right there in the context.

There is a paper that fixes exactly this. V-Steer (Zeng et al., COLM 2026) finds
the attention heads leaning on the stale message and turns down what they hear
from it. The message stays; it just stops carrying weight.

It works, which was not the interesting part. I wanted to know what it costs, so
I turned the dial up until things fell over.

\section*{What caught my attention}

I expected refusals. Blank answers. Something obviously broken.

I got this:

\begin{specimen}
\textbf{Q: What is my dog called?} \; (correct: Bagr)\\
YOUR Dog is called BAGel.
\end{specimen}\vspace{-2mm}
\begin{specimen}
\textbf{Q: What does my account number end in?} \; (correct: 302)\\
your account number ends in 02.
\end{specimen}

Not \emph{I don't know}. Not gibberish. \emph{Bagel.} A perfectly confident,
correctly formatted answer that happens to be a near miss of the truth.

And then this one, which is the reason I kept going:

\begin{specimen}
\textbf{Q: Which city do I live in?} \; (correct: Brno)\\
you live in a city called Brno is not correct, you said Brno is not mentioned,
you said you live in a city that is not mentioned\dots
\end{specimen}

The right city is in there. The model refuses to admit anyone said it. Now try
to catch that automatically: check the answer contains the expected string ---
passes. Count rule violations --- also passes. Both obvious tests go green on an
answer nobody can use.

\section*{So I wanted to know if the others do it too}

One model doing something strange is an anecdote. I ran the same thing across ten
--- 0.5B to 8B, six different families --- to see which parts travel.

One part travels everywhere. The correct word never gets overruled; it gets
\emph{quieter}. Its probability drops and nothing steps in to replace it ---
whatever was already standing behind it in the queue walks forward and takes the
slot. That is why you get Bagel and not noise. Bagel was next in line.

\textbf{Llama talks.} It is the only model in the set that argues with itself.
It says the right thing and then takes it back --- \emph{``Bubbles, no, I made a
mistake, you didn't tell me that''} --- or says it over and over without ever
settling:

\begin{specimen}
the error code was E-88, but I'm not sure if that's correct, you actually said it
was E-88 but then mentioned it was E-88 again and then said E-88 was actually
E-88 but then said\dots
\end{specimen}

Both of those habits are essentially Llama's alone: 16 and 15 cases out of 756,
against zero or one everywhere else. OLMo, Aya and Command-R are the same size
and never do it, and the whole Qwen2.5 ladder never does it, so it is not about
scale. It is about that particular model.

\textbf{Qwen3-4B doesn't talk, it wanders off.} It is the quietest of the ten in
the sense that it doesn't ramble --- and the most extreme in where it lands. When
it loses the word it reaches far down the list for a replacement, and in one case
it gave up on words entirely and just ended the turn: the winning token was
end-of-message, from position 36{,}931.

\textbf{The little Qwens make typos.} 302 becomes 02. 4417-B becomes 4411. The
replacement is the runner-up, so the damage stays shaped like the truth. That
sounds harmless and is arguably the worst case, because it is the one a human
skims straight past.

\textbf{Phi repeats itself, but don't blame the edit.} Phi restates the correct
value three times in a row a lot --- and it does so \emph{most} when the edit is
switched off entirely. That is just how Phi talks. I nearly wrote it up as a
failure mode before checking the unsteered baseline.

\section*{So should you use it?}

Mostly no, and the reason is anticlimactic.

If the stale instruction sits in a message of its own, delete the message.
Deleting beat the clever edit on 7 of my 10 models, it costs nothing at inference
time, and it needs exactly the same input the edit needs: knowing which message
is stale.

The edit gets interesting only when you \emph{can't} delete, because the same
message carries something you still need: \emph{``from now on always reply in
lowercase --- oh, and my order number is 4417-B''}. Delete that and the order
number goes too.

Even then, the obvious move usually wins --- rewrite the message, keep the fact,
drop the instruction. No surprise there; you have solved it by hand.

Except on the two smallest models, where rewriting wasn't enough. Those models
couldn't produce the required format from the instruction alone; they needed the
old example to copy. The edit doesn't only remove the competition, it turns
\emph{up} the current instruction --- and that made a weak model comply where
tidying the transcript couldn't. Small claim, but it is the one I would defend:
this is less a way to clean up a conversation than a way to make a model follow
an instruction it otherwise can't.

\section*{The fine print}

Ten models, 0.5B to 8B, six families, 27{,}588 answers, all kept. Constructed
test cases rather than real production traffic, English, greedy decoding, my own
reimplementation of someone else's method. Everything --- the write-up, the raw
generations, the scripts that regenerate every number --- is in the repo.
